# Fundraising rules

[Back to the list](../README.md) · [Interactive version](https://danissimov.github.io/global-health-funding-opportunities/#rules)

Most first grants are won by people who prepared well, asked one good question, and followed up politely. None of this is secret, but it is rarely written down. These rules fit clinicians and faculty applying on top of a full workload.

## Before you contact anyone

- **Read the whole call first.** Read the call, its FAQ and the list of past awards. Never ask a question the page already answers: it is the fastest way to lose a programme officer's attention.
- **Check you are eligible on paper.** Country, institution type, degree, career stage, and whether you may lead or only partner. Your country page in this atlas lists the funder rules you fall under.
- **Know your ask.** Amount, duration, and what the money pays for, in one line. Keep your project profile ready (template: examples/project-profile-template.md).
- **Look at who they funded.** Find one or two past awards like yours in the funder's grants database. Mention them: it shows you understand what they fund.
- **Tell your institution early.** Head of department, finance and the ethics committee. Many calls need an institutional signature, a letter of support or internal pre-selection.

## Who to contact

- **The programme officer named in the call.** Not the CEO and not a general "info@" inbox. If no name is given, use the contact address in the call.
- **Past grantees in your country or field.** They know what reviewers want. Some will read a draft, join as co-applicant, or introduce you.
- **Your national council or university grants office.** They know internal deadlines, quotas and pre-selection rounds that are not on the funder's page.
- **A partner abroad only when it adds something.** A partner in another country should bring a method, training or equipment, not only a famous name. Check whether the call requires, allows or penalises one.
- **Warm beats cold.** An introduction from a mentor, colleague or conference contact gets read. Ask for it in one line, with your short email already written.

## What to ask, and what not to

- **Is my project in scope?** "Is a project that [one sentence with one number] within scope for this call?"
- **Can I lead from my country and institution?** "Can a [government hospital / university] in [country] apply as lead, or only as a partner?"
- **Are there budget rules not in the guidance?** Indirect-cost cap, equipment limits, salaries for existing staff, currency.
- **Why do strong applications fail?** "What was the most common reason good applications were not funded last round?"
- **Is there an early stage?** "Is there a letter of intent or concept-note stage, and do you give feedback on it?"
- **If we miss it, when is the next round?** Useful for planning, and it shows you are serious.
- **Do not ask.** "Can you fund us?", "What do you fund?", "Can we have a call to present our organisation?", or anything answered on the call page.

## How to write the email

- **A subject that says everything.** "Query: [call name] – [topic], [country]". Programme officers sort by subject.
- **Under 150 words.** Who you are (one line), the project (two lines with one number), one or two specific questions. Nothing else.
- **One email, one ask.** Do not combine a scope question, a partnership request and a meeting request.
- **Small attachments or none.** At most a one-page concept note, and only if the call allows it. Keep files under 1 MB.
- **Send from your institutional address.** Full name, role, institution, city and country in the signature.

## When you apply

- **Register weeks ahead.** SAM.gov and a UEI number, eRA Commons, an EU PIC number and funder portals can each take weeks.
- **Set your own deadline 5 working days early.** Portals slow down on deadline day. Check the time zone: "11:30 AM Pacific" is evening or night in most of the ten countries.
- **Follow the scoring criteria.** Answer them in the order listed, using the funder's words as headings.
- **Every budget line tied to an activity.** State each assumption and check every cap. Reviewers read the budget as evidence that you can deliver.
- **Get a hostile read.** Ask someone who did not write it to act as Reviewer #2. AI can do a first pass; a colleague must do the second.
- **Apply to several.** Success rates are often below 20%, and lower at big foundations. Reuse your profile and keep a tracker.
- **Never pay to apply.** Real funders do not charge application fees. Be wary of "grant agents" who ask for money up front.

## Keep the relationship

- **Be honest about gaps.** Do not overstate capacity. Funders talk to each other, and gaps you name can become budget lines.
- **Deliver reports on time.** Your reputation with one funder is the strongest part of your next application.
- **Credit partners and local staff.** In reports, papers and talks.
- **No patient data in emails or AI chats.** Aggregates only, always.

## How often to follow up

| When                                    | What to do                                                                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Day 0                                   | First email: one clear question.                                                                                                                                                |
| After 7–10 working days                 | One polite follow-up in the same thread. Repeat the question in one line. Example: "Following up on my question below: is [project] within scope? A one-line answer is enough." |
| After about 3 weeks                     | Try one other channel: the call inbox, a funder webinar's Q&A, or an introduction. Then stop. Silence is an answer: apply anyway if you are eligible.                           |
| Within 1–2 working days of any reply    | Say thank you and do what they advised. Do not argue with the answer.                                                                                                           |
| Last 5 working days before the deadline | No new questions. Many funders stop answering, and it signals late planning.                                                                                                    |
| After a decision                        | Funded: thank them and report on time. Not funded: within 2 weeks, ask for reviewer comments and whether they welcome a resubmission.                                           |
| Between rounds                          | At most 2–3 short updates a year to people you have spoken with: a result, a paper, a milestone. Not a newsletter.                                                              |

## Email templates

### First email to a programme officer

```text
Subject: Query: [call name] – [topic], [country]

Dear [name],

I am [role] at [institution], [city, country]. We plan to apply to [call name] with a project that [one sentence with one number, e.g. "cuts TB drug-resistance results from 3 weeks to 2 days"].

Before we start, one question: [your one specific question that the call page does not answer]?

A one-line answer would help us a lot. Thank you for your time.

[Full name]
[Role, institution, city, country]
[Institutional email]
```

### Follow-up after 7–10 working days

```text
Subject: Re: Query: [call name] – [topic], [country]

Dear [name],

Following up on my question below: [question in one line]. A short answer is enough.

Thank you,
[Name]
```

### After a rejection

```text
Subject: [application number] – request for reviewer feedback

Dear [name],

Thank you for considering our application [title]. We would value the reviewers' comments so we can improve it. Would a revised application be welcome in a future round?

With thanks,
[Name]
```
