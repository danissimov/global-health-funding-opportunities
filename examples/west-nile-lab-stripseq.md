## Project 1: StripSeq

> Written for: Gates Foundation Grand Challenges, *Innovations in Low-Cost and Simplified Pathogen Sequencing Workflows* (closed 29 Sep 2026; kept here as a worked example).

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Title | StripSeq: one-pot, freeze-dried amplicon sequencing from used malaria rapid tests |
| Stage | Preliminary data on 40 archived strips; no finished workflow |
| Ask | Tier 1, US$300,000, 24 months |
| Pathogens | *P. falciparum* (year 1); *M. tuberculosis* panel (year 2) |
| Targets | kelch13, crt, mdr1 (drug resistance); hrp2/hrp3 deletions (false-negative rapid tests) |
| Platform | Existing MinION Mk1B; no new sequencer |

### Problem

About 40,000 used malaria rapid tests are thrown away every month. Two threats are growing in the region: artemisinin partial resistance (kelch13 mutations) and hrp2/3 deletions, which make rapid tests miss infections. Nobody tracks either routinely, because sequencing one sample costs about US$78, needs a −80 °C freezer and takes a full day of hands-on work.

### What we will do

1. Health-centre staff drop used rapid-test strips into a labelled envelope. Shipped at room temperature on the existing sample-transport network. No blood draw, no cold chain.
2. In the lab, the strip goes straight into one tube of freeze-dried reagents that amplifies all targets. No separate extraction. About 30 minutes hands-on per batch.
3. Barcoded amplicons run on the existing MinION. An offline analysis tool prints a one-page resistance report.
4. Year 2: the same backbone takes a swappable TB drug-resistance panel, cutting turnaround from 14–21 days to under 48 hours.

### Sustainability

Results feed the national malaria programme's decision on which rapid tests to buy. WHO guidance recommends switching away from hrp2-only tests when deletions cause more than 5% false negatives, which gives the ministry a direct reason to keep paying for surveillance after the grant.
