    How to search: a six-step routine

    The method behind this atlas, so you can repeat it for your own topic.

    1. Know which lists you are on

      Check your World Bank income group (reset every 1 July), OECD DAC status, Commonwealth and Horizon Europe association, EDCTP membership and Wellcome's country list. Most eligibility rules are written against these lists.

    2. Map who already funds health at home

      Open IATI d-portal or the OECD CRS for your country and filter to health. The funders and implementers you see there are your likeliest partners and sub-grantors.

    3. Find people funded for similar work

      Search NIH RePORTER, Dimensions, Wellcome's funded grants and 360Giving GrantNav by topic and country. Funded PIs become mentors, co-applicants and letter writers.

    4. Subscribe to five feeds, not fifty

      TDR newsletter, Fogarty funding news, The Global Health Network, one aggregator (fundsforNGOs or Opportunity Desk), and your national council's call page.

    5. Let AI sweep, then verify every line

      Run Prompt 1 to build a long list quickly. Then open each official page: deadlines, amounts and eligible countries are exactly where models make mistakes.

    6. Track it and register early

      Keep a tracker with deadline, eligibility check and next action. US federal applications need SAM.gov, NCAGE and eRA Commons registration, which takes weeks. Start now for the 3 Dec K43 deadline.

    An AI-native project description

    Write your project down once, in plain text with facts and numbers. Paste it into every grant chat instead of retyping it.

    Facts, not prose

    Headcount, equipment, sample volumes, costs, dates. Funders and AI both score on numbers.

    One file, many grants

    The same profile answers eligibility, budget, capacity and sustainability questions for every call.

    Say what you do not have

    Gaps (power, freezers, a statistician) are where partners and budget lines come from.

    No patient data

    Aggregate numbers only. Nothing that identifies a person.

    Example: StripSeq, northern Uganda

    Fictional lab, written for the Gates Foundation call on low-cost pathogen sequencing. This is the short version that fits on one slide.

    Example (fictional) · West Nile Regional Laboratory, Arua, Uganda

    One idle Nanopore sequencer, 4 technicians, unreliable power. TB drug-resistance results take 2–3 weeks from Kampala.

    Problem. 40,000 malaria rapid tests are thrown away every month. Nobody tracks artemisinin resistance or hrp2/3 deletions (which cause false-negative tests), because sequencing costs about $80 per sample.

    Project: “StripSeq”

    1. Used rapid-test strip → envelope, no cold chain
    2. One tube of freeze-dried reagents, no extraction, 30 min hands-on
    3. Existing sequencer → one-page resistance report
    4. Same workflow, swap in a TB panel in year 2 (results in under 48 h)
    Cost
    $78 → about $6 per sample
    Ask
    Tier 1, $300k, 24 months
    Partners
    National malaria programme, local university, Africa Pathogen Genomics Initiative
    Month 12
    500 strips, ≥95% agreement with the gold standard

    Method. Five research streams (national, multilateral and regional, foreign government, foundations and corporate, fellowships and search tools) collected candidates, then each entry was checked on the funder's own page or a reputable tracker in September 2026. Income groups follow the World Bank FY2027 classification; Wellcome country rules follow its published list for rounds opening 29 Oct 2026; US agreement status follows the KFF tracker (data as of 11 Sep 2026).
